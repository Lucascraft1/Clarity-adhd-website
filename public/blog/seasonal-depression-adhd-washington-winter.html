Is It ADHD or the Washington Winter? Seasonal Depression, Sleep Timing, and Focus

Lucas Craft, MMS, PA-C  ·  Published December 1, 2026  ·  Washington State
← Back to blog

Quick answer: Trouble concentrating can come from ADHD, from seasonal depression, or from both, and the two overlap more than chance would predict. The clearest clue is timing: ADHD is a long-standing pattern, while seasonal depression comes back at the same time of year, typically late fall into winter, and lifts in spring. Late sleep timing may be part of what connects them. Morning light therapy is an established option for winter-pattern seasonal depression, but the evidence that it treats ADHD itself is thin. If this sounds familiar, it is worth sorting out with a clinician rather than guessing, and if you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988.

Once the clocks fall back, a familiar question comes up for a lot of adults with ADHD. Focus has fallen apart, the to-do list has stalled, getting out of bed takes everything, and they want to know whether their ADHD is getting worse, their medication has stopped working, or something else is going on.

Sometimes it is the ADHD. Sometimes it is the season. Often it is a bit of both, and the answer changes what helps. This post is about that seasonal question specifically: what the research says about seasonal depression in adults with ADHD, where sleep timing fits, and how far the light therapy evidence actually goes.

Why Winter Is a Fair Question in Washington

The National Institute of Mental Health describes seasonal affective disorder (SAD) as a type of depression with a recurring seasonal pattern. In most cases it starts in late fall or early winter and eases in spring and summer, and NIMH notes that the mood changes are tied to shorter daylight hours rather than to the calendar or the stress of the holidays.1 NIMH also notes that SAD is more common in people living farther north, where winter days are shorter.1 Washington is about as far north as the lower 48 gets, so the question is a reasonable one to ask here.

Not every gray-season slump is SAD. NIMH describes the diagnosis as depressive episodes that come back in a specific season for at least two consecutive years, and that happen more often in that season than at other times of year.1 A hard February is not a diagnosis. A pattern that keeps returning is worth a closer look.

Alongside the usual depression symptoms, NIMH lists features that are more typical of the winter pattern: oversleeping, overeating with carbohydrate cravings, and social withdrawal that feels like hibernating.1 Difficulty concentrating is on the depression list too, which is exactly why winter can look like an ADHD problem from the inside.

What the Research Says About ADHD and Seasonal Depression

NIMH notes that people with SAD tend to have other conditions, and lists ADHD among them.1 The studies that looked from the ADHD side point the same way.

In a Dutch study of 2,239 adults, probable SAD showed up in 9.9% of people with clinically significant ADHD symptoms, compared with 3.3% of those without. The link held after adjusting for current depression and anxiety, age, sex, education, and antidepressant and benzodiazepine use.2 An earlier study of adults with ADHD who also had a current or past mood disorder estimated that 27% had SAD, with women at higher risk than men.3 That second group was already selected for mood problems, so the number is not a rate for adults with ADHD in general.

Both studies used a screening questionnaire for seasonality rather than a full diagnostic interview, and the authors of the 27% study say so directly.3 "Probable SAD" in research is a flag, not a diagnosis. Still, the overlap shows up often enough that seasons are worth asking about in any adult ADHD evaluation.

Where Sleep Timing Comes In

The most interesting finding in the Dutch study was not the overlap itself but what seemed to connect it. Later self-reported sleep onset was an important statistical link between ADHD symptoms and probable SAD, even after accounting for other factors.2 A separate study comparing 202 adult ADHD patients with 189 controls found that seasonal depressive symptoms went along with later sleep, among other factors.4

These are associations from questionnaire data, not proof of cause. But they fit what many adults with ADHD already know about their own body clock. I have written about that delayed rhythm, and how hard it is to shift, in ADHD and sleep in adults. The seasonal angle adds one thing: when daylight is short and a clock already runs late, winter mornings can be especially rough.

Telling a Seasonal Pattern From Year-Round ADHD

I am not going to give you a checklist that sorts one from the other, because there is not a reliable one and I would not diagnose either from a blog post. What I can tell you is what I pay attention to.

Timeline matters most. ADHD is a long-standing pattern that shows up across seasons, years, and settings, usually with a history going back well before adulthood. Seasonal depression has a shape: a change from your usual self that tends to arrive in the darker months and lift when the days lengthen. If your focus is fine in July and falls apart every November, that is a different story from focus that has been hard every month of your life.

The two can also stack. Someone with lifelong ADHD can develop a seasonal mood pattern on top of it, and then winter feels like the ADHD got worse when something new has joined it. If you are on ADHD medication and it seems to fade every winter, that is worth naming at a visit rather than raising the dose on your own. Why ADHD medication can seem to stop working walks through the other common reasons.

The broader question of how depression and ADHD get confused, in any season, is covered in ADHD and depression in adults, and the wider list of conditions that can imitate ADHD is in the ADHD mimics post.

What Light Therapy Can and Cannot Claim

For winter-pattern seasonal depression, light therapy is an established treatment. NIMH describes the standard approach as sitting in front of a 10,000 lux light box for about 30 to 45 minutes a day, usually first thing in the morning, from fall to spring.1 A 2005 meta-analysis of randomized trials found that bright light reduced depression severity in SAD with effects comparable to those in many antidepressant trials,5 and a 2020 meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials also found it more effective than placebo, while noting that many of the studies were small, varied in design, and carried a moderate to high risk of bias.6 NIMH also describes a direct comparison in which CBT adapted for SAD worked about as well as light therapy, with some evidence that the benefits of CBT lasted longer.1

For ADHD itself, the evidence is much thinner. A small open trial of morning light in 29 adults with ADHD during fall and winter reported improvements in ADHD and mood symptoms, with the strongest predictor being a shift toward an earlier body-clock preference on a questionnaire.7 There was no comparison group, so it cannot show that the light caused the change. A later randomized trial in 49 adults with ADHD and delayed sleep phase found that low-dose melatonin plus morning bright light moved the internal clock earlier but did not improve ADHD symptoms, and did not move actual sleep times either.8

So my honest summary is this. Light therapy is a reasonable option to discuss if you have a seasonal depression pattern. It is not an established treatment for ADHD, and it should not replace an evaluation or a treatment plan that is working.

Before You Buy a Light Box

Light boxes are sold without a prescription, but that does not make them right for everyone. NIMH notes that people with certain eye diseases, or who take medications that increase sensitivity to sunlight, may need to use light therapy under medical supervision or choose another treatment.1 NIMH also notes that SAD is more common in people with bipolar disorder, especially bipolar II.1 A 2020 meta-analysis found that light therapy studied as an add-on to bipolar treatment did not raise the risk of mood switching, but the trials were small and short.9 If you have bipolar disorder, or a history of high-energy stretches with little need for sleep, light therapy belongs in a conversation with the clinician managing your mood, not a solo experiment. ADHD and bipolar disorder covers why that history matters.

NIMH lists other options for SAD as well: psychotherapy including CBT adapted for SAD, antidepressant medication, and vitamin D, which has produced mixed results in studies.1 Which one fits depends on your history, and that is a decision to make with a clinician who knows it.

What I Would Want You to Do With This

If winter reliably takes your focus and mood with it, write down when it starts and when it lifts, how you are sleeping, and what changes. That timeline is more useful at a visit than any single symptom.

If your attention has been hard in every season for as long as you can remember, and winter just makes it worse, that is a reason to look at ADHD carefully rather than blaming the weather. A structured evaluation can look at both.

And if winter has moved past a slump into hopelessness, or you are having thoughts of death or suicide, please do not wait for a scheduled appointment. Call or text the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline at 988, or call 911 in an emergency.1
