Paying for ADHD Care With an HSA or FSA in Washington: What Counts and When

Lucas Craft, MMS, PA-C  ·  Published November 3, 2026  ·  Washington State
← Back to blog

Quick answer: HSA and FSA money can usually pay for ADHD evaluation, follow-up visits, and prescribed ADHD medication, because the IRS treats diagnosis, treatment, and prescribed drugs as medical care. The details depend on your plan. The big difference is timing: an FSA reimburses care based on the date you received it, not the date you paid, and many FSAs run on the calendar year. What Usually Counts

The IRS defines medical care broadly: the costs of diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, including payments for legal medical services by physicians and other medical practitioners.1,2 Prescribed medicines and drugs are listed explicitly as medical expenses.1 In practice, that generally covers:

  • An ADHD evaluation, including objective testing like QbCheck, because it is diagnostic care.
  • Follow-up visits for medication management.
  • ADHD medication filled with a prescription, whether stimulant or non-stimulant.

Two caveats. First, for an FSA, qualified expenses are the ones your plan specifies, which are generally the expenses that would qualify for the medical deduction.3 Most plans follow the IRS definition closely, but your plan document is the rulebook. Second, I cannot tell you whether a particular charge will be approved by your administrator. If you are unsure, ask them before you spend money you are counting on getting back.

HSA vs. FSA: The Difference That Matters Here

People use the two terms interchangeably, but they behave very differently.

HSA (Health Savings Account)

  • You can only contribute if you are covered by a qualifying high-deductible health plan and meet the other eligibility rules.3
  • The money is yours. Contributions remain in the account until you use them, and the account stays with you if you change jobs.3
  • Distributions are tax-free when they pay for qualified medical expenses you incur after you establish the HSA.3 Care from before the account was opened does not qualify.
  • For 2026, the contribution limit is $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, plus an extra $1,000 if you are 55 or older.3

There is no year-end deadline to spend HSA money. The main HSA task is record keeping: the IRS expects you to keep records showing the money went to qualified medical expenses that were not reimbursed from another source.3 Keep your receipts and superbills.

FSA (Health Flexible Spending Arrangement)

  • FSAs are set up through an employer. For 2026, the most you can contribute through salary reduction is $3,400.4
  • FSAs are generally use-it-or-lose-it. Money left at the end of the plan year is forfeited unless your plan offers one of two exceptions: a grace period of up to two and a half months after the plan year ends, or a carryover.3 A plan can offer one or the other, not both.
  • For 2026 plan years, the maximum carryover is $680.4 Your plan may allow less, or none.
  • The FSA can only reimburse expenses incurred during your period of coverage, and it cannot make advance reimbursements of future or projected expenses.3

FSA Timing: Date of Care, Not Date of Payment

This is the part most people get wrong, and it matters more for FSAs than anything else in this post.

Under the Treasury regulations on cafeteria plans, medical expenses are incurred when you are provided with the care, not when you are billed, charged, or pay for it.5 That has a few practical consequences:

  • Paying in December for care in January does not move the care into December. If your FSA plan year ends December 31, a visit on January 8 is a next-year expense, no matter when you paid.
  • Paying later does not disqualify care that already happened. A visit on December 15 is a this-year expense even if a payment for it lands in January. The FSA rules allow reimbursement after the coverage period ends, as long as the care happened during it.5 Your plan will have a deadline for submitting claims, so check it.
  • The IRS rule for tax deductions is different. For the itemized medical deduction, expenses generally count in the year you pay them. Publication 502 says outright that this is not the rule for FSA reimbursement.1 Do not mix the two up.

Here is how this applies to care with me. The evaluation and treatment program is 5 visits over 5 weeks, followed by a 2-week trial of an extended-release medication, so about 7 weeks from start to finish. The program fee is $1,099, and you can pay it in full or split it into 3 or 4 monthly payments at no extra cost. Neither payment schedule lines up exactly with the visit schedule, and from the FSA's point of view, the visits are what count.

So if you are starting in November or December with calendar-year FSA money, the visits that happen before December 31 are the ones that can generally be reimbursed from this year's FSA. Visits after that date belong to the next plan year, unless your plan has a grace period that covers them. If you are paying a lump sum up front with an FSA card, your administrator may ask for documentation showing dates of service. Superbills from each visit list the date of service, which is usually what they want.

If this is confusing for your situation, call your FSA administrator before you pay and ask: "Does my plan have a grace period or carryover, and what is the deadline to submit claims?" That one call answers most of it.

Planning for Next Year

Open enrollment is also when people set next year's FSA election. If you are already in care, ongoing visits are $175, typically every one to three months, with $225 extended visits when something more complex comes up. Most established patients spend about $700 a year with the practice. Pharmacy costs are separate and vary a lot by medication and pharmacy, so add those too. Because unused FSA money is generally forfeited above any carryover, it is usually safer to elect what you are confident you will use than to guess high.

Superbills and Out-of-Network Reimbursement

Because I do not bill insurance, I provide a superbill after each visit: an itemized receipt with diagnosis and procedure codes that you can submit to your insurer yourself for possible out-of-network reimbursement.

Be realistic about what a superbill is. It is documentation, not a guarantee. Whether your insurer pays anything back depends entirely on your plan. Some plans have out-of-network benefits and some do not, and some plans apply out-of-network claims to a separate deductible first. Call the member services number on your card and ask whether you have out-of-network outpatient benefits and how much of your out-of-network deductible remains.

One more thing to watch: if you pay for a visit with HSA or FSA money and your insurer later reimburses you for the same visit, the same expense has now been paid twice. FSAs require a statement that an expense has not been reimbursed under other coverage, and HSA records are supposed to show expenses were not reimbursed from another source.3 If that happens, ask your administrator or a tax professional how to correct it.

Paying Without Insurance? You Can Ask for a Good Faith Estimate

If you are not using health insurance to pay for care, federal rules generally require providers to give you a good faith estimate of expected charges if you request one or schedule care at least 3 business days in advance.6 That applies to cash-pay ADHD care too. It is a useful document to have when you are planning how much of a year's FSA election or HSA balance to set aside.

The Short Version

  • ADHD evaluation, follow-up visits, and prescribed ADHD medication generally count as medical care, but your plan has the final word.
  • HSA money does not expire. FSA money usually does, unless your plan has a grace period or a carryover (up to $680 for 2026 plan years).
  • FSAs follow the date you received care, not the date you paid.
  • Superbills support out-of-network claims, but reimbursement depends on your plan.
  • Keep every receipt and superbill.
